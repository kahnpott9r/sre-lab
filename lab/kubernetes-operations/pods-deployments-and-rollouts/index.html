Lesson 02

Pods, Deployments and rollouts

A Deployment is a versioned history of ReplicaSets. Understanding that ownership chain is what makes rollbacks safe.

25m1 lab

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Trace a Deployment down through ReplicaSet to Pod to container
  • Describe how a rolling update maintains availability
  • Read rollout history and choose a safe rollback target
  • Explain maxSurge and maxUnavailable in terms of capacity

One chain, four levels

Deployment  web
│  desired: 4 replicas, image app:1.4, strategy RollingUpdate
│
└── ReplicaSet  web-7d9f4c8b6      (revision 12)
    │  desired: 4 pods
    │
    └── Pod  web-7d9f4c8b6-x2k4p
        │  node: worker-2, phase: Running
        │
        └── Container  web        (image app:1.4)

Each level owns the one below and applies its own reconciliation. When something is wrong, the debugging move is to walk up until the spec looks right, then down again:

A change to the Deployment creates a new ReplicaSet; it never mutates the existing Pods. That is deliberate:

  • Pods get new names and new nodes — a rollout is a replacement, not a patch.
  • The old ReplicaSet stays behind with its replica count reduced to zero.
  • Rolling back is scaling that old ReplicaSet back up: seconds, not a rebuild.

Watch out

rollout undo with no --to-revision goes back exactly one step. If the last two deploys were both bad, that targets another bad revision. Always read the history and name the revision you actually trust.

How a rolling update stays available

Two numbers in the strategy control the arithmetic:

Think of it as capacity, not pod counts:

SettingPeak pods (replicas=4)Capacity during rollout
maxSurge: 1, maxUnavailable: 05Never below 4 — no dip
maxSurge: 0, maxUnavailable: 14Dips to 3 — cheaper, slightly riskier
maxSurge: 25%, maxUnavailable: 25%5Dips to 3 — the Kubernetes default

maxUnavailable: 0 is the correct default for anything user-facing: you pay for one extra pod during the deploy and never lose serving capacity. For a batch worker where a brief dip is fine, trading capacity for cost is reasonable.

The rollout advances in strict order: create new Pod → wait for Ready → remove an old Pod → repeat. That ordering is why a failing readiness probe stops everything, and why the sequence is safe by construction.

Readiness and liveness are different questions

These are two probes answering two different questions, and swapping them is a common production foot-gun:

ProbeQuestionFailure consequence
readinessCan this instance serve traffic right now?Removed from Service endpoints. Container keeps running
livenessIs this process wedged beyond recovery?Container is restarted
startupHas it finished initialising?Liveness/readiness are suppressed until it passes

A readiness probe is the right tool for warm-up and for shedding load during a temporary slowdown — the pod stops receiving requests but keeps its state and recovers on its own. A liveness probe is a last resort.

Watch out

An aggressive liveness probe converts a slow period into an outage. If a container needs 30 seconds to warm a cache and the liveness probe fires at 10, the pod is killed mid-warm-up, restarts, and is killed again. Use a startup probe for slow initialisation and leave liveness for genuinely stuck processes.

Why "Running but not Ready" stalls a deploy

The event sequence is worth internalising, because it looks alarming and is actually correct behaviour:

  1. New ReplicaSet is created with 1 replica (maxSurge: 1).
  2. Pod starts; container enters Running.
  3. Readiness probe fails, so the Pod stays not Ready.
  4. The rollout will not remove an old Pod while maxUnavailable: 0 — that would reduce available capacity.
  5. kubectl rollout status blocks, indefinitely.

Nothing is broken in the controller. It is refusing to take capacity away from a service it cannot yet replace. Your bug is in the probe or the application:

If you must push through, kubectl rollout undo restores the previous ReplicaSet immediately.

Wiring a Service to the right pods

A Service finds Pods through a label selector, and it only adds Ready Pods to its endpoints. That is the mechanism that makes readness probes meaningful for traffic:

Two classic bugs live here:

  • Selector mismatch. The Service selects app=web while the Deployment labels pods app=webserver. The Service has zero endpoints and returns connection refused. kubectl get endpoints makes this obvious in one line.
  • No readiness probe. Kubernetes then assumes the container is ready the moment it starts, so traffic arrives during warm-up and users see 5xx errors for the first seconds of every deploy.

Command cheat sheet

Each entry is the syntax, a real invocation with the output to expect, the failure it hides and where it behaves differently on another system.

kubectl get deploy,rs,pods -l app=web

Watch the whole ownership chain in one view.

kubectl rollout status deploy/web

Block until the rollout converges, or report why not.

kubectl rollout history deploy/web

Every revision, with its change cause.

kubectl rollout undo deploy/web --to-revision=3

Roll back to a specific revision, not just the previous one.

kubectl set image deploy/web web=app:1.5

Trigger a new revision by changing the image.
