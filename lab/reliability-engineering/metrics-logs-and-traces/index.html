Lesson 02

Metrics, logs and traces

Three signals, three different questions. Choosing the wrong one is why you spend an hour finding something that took thirty seconds.

28m1 lab

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Match a question to the signal that can answer it
  • Understand cardinality as the cost driver in metrics
  • Write a PromQL rate correctly, including the counter-reset trap
  • Know what tracing adds that aggregation cannot

Three questions, three tools

The signals are not interchangeable. Each answers a different shape of question:

SignalAnswersCost driverStrength
MetricsHow much, how often, how fast — over timeNumber of distinct series (cardinality)Cheap to store, fast to aggregate, perfect for alerting
LogsWhat exactly happened, in detail, for a specific eventVolume of bytesHigh cardinality, full detail, expensive at scale
TracesWhere the time went, across service boundariesSampling rate and span volumeReveals causality and gaps that aggregation hides

And the workflow that uses them in the right order:

Metrics   →  WHERE is the problem?     (which endpoint, instance, region)
Traces    →  WHICH part of the request? (which span, which hop, which retry)
Logs      →  WHY, in detail?           (for those specific requests only)

Starting with logs means querying terabytes to find what a single metric label would have told you in one second. Starting with traces wastes sampling budget on a healthy system. The order is what makes observability affordable.

Tip

Metrics are for unknown questions asked repeatedly ("is anything wrong anywhere?"). Logs are for known questions asked once ("why did request abc123 fail?"). Confusing these two is the root of most observability bills.

Cardinality is the bill you did not expect

A metric is identified by its name plus every label combination. That product is your series count, and series count is roughly linear in the metrics backend's memory:

http_requests_total{service, method, status, instance}
   5 services × 4 methods × 6 statuses × 20 instances = 2,400 series   ✅ fine

http_requests_total{service, method, status, instance, user_id}
   ... × 100,000 users = 240,000,000 series                            ❌ outage

The rule is simple: labels must have bounded, low-cardinality value sets. Good labels: status, method, region, instance, version. Bad labels: user_id, request_id, email, full_url, session_id, timestamp.

If you need to slice by a high-cardinality value, that is a question for logs or traces — they are designed for it.

A related trap: putting the whole URL in a label. /users/{id} as a label is unbounded; normalise it to a route template (/users/:id) before it becomes a label.

Reading counters correctly

A counter only goes up (mostly). A gauge goes up and down. Applying the wrong function is a silent correctness bug:

rate() and increase() detect counter resets and compensate. That behaviour is essential after a deploy restarts your process — without it, every restart would produce a huge negative spike. But the same behaviour silently corrupts any gauge you feed it, because a gauge's downward movement is genuine.

increase is often what you actually want when a human will read the number: "how many errors in the last hour" is more meaningful than "1.7 per second".

Percentiles from histograms

Three things people get wrong here:

  1. You must sum by le. The le (less-than-or-equal) label defines the buckets. Dropping it produces nonsense.
  2. Percentiles are not averageable. The p95 of instance A and the p95 of instance B do not average to the p95 of the fleet. Compute from buckets.
  3. Bucket boundaries decide accuracy. If your buckets jump from 500ms to 5s, your p99 is interpolated and can be wildly wrong. Choose buckets around your SLO threshold.

Watch out

Histogram quantiles are approximations from cumulative buckets. That is acceptable for alerting and dashboards, and not acceptable for billing or capacity decisions where you need exact accounting. Know which one you are doing.

What traces add

A trace is one request's journey, as a tree of spans:

GET /checkout                       1,240ms
├── auth.verify                       12ms
├── cart.load                         31ms
├── inventory.reserve                980ms   ← the problem
│   ├── db.query                      120ms
│   └── http.retry (2 attempts)       840ms   ← the real cause
└── payment.charge                   190ms

Metrics told you checkout is slow. They could not tell you that 840 ms of the time was two retries against a struggling dependency, because each service's own metrics reported healthy p99s — the retries were invisible in aggregate.

That "everyone is fine and the user is not" situation is precisely when you need traces. It usually means time is being lost in a place nobody instruments: a connection pool wait, a DNS resolution, a retry loop, or a hop between services that has no span of its own.

Sampling and cost

Tracing every request at full fidelity is usually unaffordable at scale. The standard compromise is tail-based sampling:

  • Head sampling decides at trace start (e.g. 1%) — cheap, but it discards the interesting 100% of errors by chance.
  • Tail sampling buffers a trace and decides after seeing the whole thing, keeping all errors and all slow traces — more expensive to run, far more useful.

Configuring tail sampling to keep every error and every request above your SLO latency threshold means your trace store holds exactly the traces you will need during an incident, at a fraction of full volume.

Command cheat sheet

Each entry is the syntax, a real invocation with the output to expect, the failure it hides and where it behaves differently on another system.

rate(counter[5m])

Per-second rate over a range — handles counter resets.

histogram_quantile(0.95, sum by (le) (rate(...)))

Percentile from histogram buckets.

sum by (status) (rate(...))

Aggregate while keeping one dimension — the workhorse of dashboards.

increase(counter[1h])

Total change over a window, in the counter's own units.
