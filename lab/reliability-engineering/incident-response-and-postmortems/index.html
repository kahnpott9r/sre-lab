Lesson 03

Incident response and postmortems

An incident is a coordination problem under uncertainty. The response is a process, and the postmortem is where its value is realised.

30m1 lab

By the end of this lesson you can

  • State the roles in an incident command structure and why they are separate
  • Apply the "mitigate before you diagnose" rule
  • Distinguish contributing factors from a single root cause
  • Write action items with owners and dates, and know what makes one useless

An incident is a coordination problem

The technical fault is only part of what makes an incident hard. The rest is uncertainty plus several people acting on incomplete information. A structure exists so that the coordination does not itself become the failure:

RoleOwnsExplicitly does not
Incident CommanderDecisions, delegation, the timelineDebug anything themselves
Operations / Tech LeadHands-on investigation and changeDecide on comms or rollback policy
Communications LeadStatus updates to stakeholdersTouch production
ScribeTimeline, decisions, timestampsInvestigate
Subject-matter expertsTargeted expertise when askedRoam uninvited across the incident

The rule people resist most is the first one: the Incident Commander must not debug. Under pressure it feels wasteful to have a senior engineer not touching the system. In practice, the IC's attention is what keeps state coherent — who is doing what, what has been tried, what the current hypothesis is, whether a rollback is needed.

Watch out

Without a dedicated IC you get the classic failure mode: two engineers fixing the same problem in different ways, nobody watching the error rate, and a status update that never goes out. Everyone is busy, nothing is coordinated, and the incident runs several times longer than it needed to.

Declare early, mitigate before you diagnose

Two habits make the largest difference, and both run against instinct:

Declare early. Declaration is cheap. Not declaring costs you the structure above precisely when it matters. The threshold should be "this might be an incident", not "this is definitely worse than last time". You can always downgrade.

Mitigate before you diagnose. The first question is not "why?" — it is "what stops the bleeding?"

MitigationTypical timeReversible
Roll back the last deployseconds to minutesYes
Fail over to another regionminutesUsually
Feature-flag off the new pathsecondsYes
Scale up to absorb loadminutesYes
Restart the affected servicesecondsYes (but loses evidence)

Notice that the cause is often obvious after mitigation, because the symptom disappearing tells you a great deal. Diagnosis under a live outage is the slowest possible route to a stable service.

What to capture, and when

A useful timeline records:

  • Detection — the alert, or the customer report. Note which, because a customer report means your monitoring has a gap.
  • Actions taken and their observed effect — including the ones that did nothing.
  • Hypotheses ruled out. These are evidence, not noise. "14:20 — suspected alert thresholds; checked and ruled out" shows how triage actually went and stops the next person from repeating it.
  • Decisions and who made them — especially the choice to roll back.
  • Recovery point — when the metric returned to normal, not when the change was made. They are routinely different.

Tip

Write timestamps in UTC, and record the observation separately from the action. "We rolled back at 14:35" and "error rate returned to baseline at 14:41" are different facts, and the gap between them is often the most interesting number in the whole postmortem.

Root cause is a lie that feels satisfying

"The root cause was a typo." It is precise, it is true, and it is useless.

Every human error was preceded by a system that allowed the error to reach production and take down a service. The productive framing is contributing factors:

UtilityContributing factors that actually matter
"Typo in a config file"No schema validation in the pipeline; config applied directly to prod with no staging; no automated diff review; deploy had no canary
"The engineer ran the wrong command"A command whose blast radius was invisible; no dry-run mode; production credentials on every laptop; no approval step for destructive operations

The test for a good analysis: would this failure be possible for a competent person having a normal day? If yes, the system is the finding. That is not leniency — it is the only framing that produces changes that stick.

This is why blameless postmortems are not a courtesy. The moment an investigation is about identifying a person, everyone's incentive shifts to not being that person: details get vague, actions get delayed, and the next incident has the same shape.

Action items that change something

Sort every action item into one of three kinds, because only one of them works:

KindExampleVerdict
Intention"Be more careful with config edits"Useless. No completion, no owner, fails identically next time
Vague"Improve monitoring and add tests"Unknowable completion. Silently dropped
Structural"Config pipeline rejects unknown keys before deploy — platform team, due 3 weeks, tracked in JIRA-1234"Removes the failure mode. Verifiable, finishable, reviewable

A structural item has three properties: it removes the failure mode rather than discouraging the mistake, it has a named owner, and it has a date. Anything else is a note, not an action.

Two habits that keep action items honest:

  • Cap them. Five real items beat twenty aspirational ones. If everything is an action item, nothing is.
  • Review them at the next incident. An action item that was never done is itself a finding about prioritisation — and the most common way organisations have the same outage twice.

Review, and actually learn

Day 0    Mitigate, resolve, confirm stable
Day 0-1  Timeline written while memory is fresh
Day 1-3  Review meeting: 45 minutes, blameless, hypotheses not verdicts
Day 3-7  Action items filed with owners and dates
Ongoing  Reviewed at the next incident — were they done? did they work?

The blameless part is not the absence of accountability. It is the deliberate separation of understanding the system from evaluating people, so that the people who know most about what happened — the ones who were there — say everything they know.
