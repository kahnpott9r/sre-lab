Lesson 01

SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

Pick a number that reflects user pain, promise a level for it, and then let the remaining budget decide whether you ship.

30m1 lab

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Distinguish an SLI from an SLO from an SLA
  • Derive allowed downtime from an availability target
  • Compute an error budget and explain what it permits
  • Avoid the classic mistakes: vanity SLIs and 100% targets

Three acronyms, three different jobs

TermWhat it isExample
SLI — indicatorA measurement of user-visible behaviourProportion of requests served < 300 ms
SLO — objectiveThe internal target for that SLI99.9% over a rolling 30 days
SLA — agreementThe external contract, with penalties99.5%, with service credits below that

The order matters: the SLA must be looser than the SLO. If you promise customers 99.9% and target 99.9% internally, the first bad afternoon is a breach before it is a warning. Promise 99.5%, target 99.9%, and you get a signal with room to act.

Choosing an SLI: ratios, not resources

The single most useful rule:

A good SLI is a ratio of good events to total events, from the user's point of view.

That shape is powerful because it is bounded (0 to 1), comparable across services, and it only improves when the user's experience improves.

Compare with the SLIs people reach for first:

Weak SLIWhy it fails
CPU utilisationResource, not experience. 15% CPU with total failure passes
Memory usedSame problem, inverted
Requests per secondVolume, not health. Zero traffic looks perfect
Queue depthAn internal proxy that can be healthy while users time out
"Uptime from a ping check"The port can answer while every real request fails

Tip

Ask "if this number is bad, is a user definitely unhappy, and if it is good, is a user definitely fine?" If either direction fails, it is not an SLI — it is a resource metric, which is useful for capacity planning and useless for an SLO.

Latency SLIs need a threshold and a percentile

"Average latency" is nearly useless: one 30-second outlier disappears into the mean while thousands of users experience it. Use a percentile with a threshold so it becomes a ratio:

Two details that matter:

  • Percentiles cannot be averaged. A p99 across instances is not the average of each instance's p99 — you must compute it from the histogram buckets, which is why histogram_quantile operates on _bucket series.
  • The tail is the user experience. Which users? The unluckiest ones. A p99 of 300 ms means one in a hundred requests is slower than that, and heavy users hit it constantly.

The error budget is the whole point

An SLO defines budget by subtraction:

99.9% over 30 days  →  0.1% of 720 hours  →  43.2 minutes of allowed failure
SLODowntime per 30 daysPer year
99%7h 12m3.65 days
99.9%43m 12s8h 46m
99.95%21m 36s4h 23m
99.99%4m 19s52m 36s
99.999%26s5m 15s

Each nine costs roughly ten times more engineering than the one before it — and usually requires removing single points of failure, not adding monitoring.

The budget turns reliability from an argument into arithmetic:

  • Budget remaining → ship features faster. The risk is affordable.
  • Budget exhausted → freeze risky change and spend the time on reliability.
  • Budget exhausted repeatedly → your SLO is wrong, or you have a structural problem. Both are informative.

That is the deal that makes SRE work politically: reliability work is funded by a number everyone agreed on in advance, not by whoever is loudest after the last outage.

Burn rate: how fast, not just how much

A budget tells you the total; the burn rate tells you the velocity. Burn rate 1.0 means you are consuming exactly on pace to exhaust the budget by the end of the window.

The reason you need multiple windows is a classic false-positive trap:

DetectorPurpose
Long window (1h or 6h)Confirms it is sustained, not a blip
Short window (5m)Confirms it is still happening, so you can resolve the alert

Alerting on a single long window is slow; alerting on a single short window pages you for noise. Requiring both gives fast detection without waking people up for a thirty-second spike.

Common thresholds: a fast burn (14.4x over 1h) means the budget is gone in about two days and pages immediately; a slow burn (6x over 6h) is a ticket.
